PUBLISHED THEOREM2026 journal publication · 2024 preprint

Guth–Maynard: finding regularity in shorter intervals

Larry Guth and James Maynard · New large value estimates for Dirichlet polynomials · Annals of Mathematics 203 (2026)

In plain language. Imagine zooming into a small neighborhood of a very large number. The usual Prime Number Theorem describes broad averages. Can you zoom in further and still reliably predict roughly how many primes are present? This work improves how far that zoom can go in a uniform asymptotic counting result.

What the mathematics changes. The paper improves bounds on large values of Dirichlet polynomials and, as a consequence, bounds on how many zeta zeros can lie far to the right. These zero-density estimates imply a prime number theorem in intervals of length xθ for every fixed θ > 17/30 ≈ 0.5667, for all sufficiently large x. This improves the classical 7/12 ≈ 0.5833 threshold for such asymptotic counts.

Advanced note: what is being estimated?

A Dirichlet polynomial is a finite sum involving terms nit and coefficients. Bounding how often its magnitude becomes large feeds into analytic estimates for ζ(s). A zero-density bound counts zeros with real part at least σ and height up to T; it is different from proving that every nontrivial zero lies on the line Re(s) = 1/2.

What it does not prove: the Riemann Hypothesis, twin primes, or a formula for the next prime. An asymptotic count in every interval is stronger than merely finding one prime there; do not compare exponent records for those two tasks as if they were the same.

Journal record · Author preprint and version history. Prerequisites: prime counting, then zeta and its zeros.

PUBLISHED THEOREMMay 12, 2026

Gafni–Tao: measuring the exceptional intervals

Ayla Gafni and Terence Tao · On the number of exceptional intervals to the prime number theorem in short intervals · Essential Number Theory 5(2), 221–241

The next question. If a theorem cannot guarantee the expected prime count in every very short interval, how many intervals can fail? “Almost all” means the exceptional fraction tends to zero; it does not mean there are no exceptions.

The contribution. The authors give an explicit relation between zero-density estimates and upper bounds on the exceptional set. This makes it possible to apply newer estimates, including Guth–Maynard's, with a small amount of computer assistance. It is a refinement of how we quantify irregularity, not a list of every exceptional interval.

Keep the two thresholds separate

The paper's abstract distinguishes the result for all sufficiently large x when θ > 17/30 from the result for almost all x when θ > 2/15. The smaller exponent comes with the weaker “almost all” quantifier. Dropping that qualifier would turn a true statement into a much stronger unsupported claim.

Publisher abstract, publication date, and paper · DOI: 10.2140/ent.2026.5.221. This is follow-up progress, not a resolution of all short-interval questions.

VERIFIED PRIME DISCOVERYOctober 12, 2024

GIMPS: a 41,024,320-digit prime, found with cloud GPUs

Luke Durant and GIMPS · 2136,279,841 − 1

What was discovered. A new Mersenne prime, the 52nd known at announcement, became the largest known prime. The PrimePages list checked on October 7, 2026 still places it first.

How evidence became proof. An A100 GPU in Dublin reported a probable-prime result on October 11. An H100 in San Antonio ran a Lucas-Lehmer proof on October 12. Independent programs and hardware then checked the result. The project report describes thousands of GPUs in 24 datacenter regions across 17 countries.

Why it matters. It demonstrates advances in large-integer arithmetic, distributed coordination, GPU computing, and verification. The point is not that everyday cryptography suddenly needs 41-million-digit primes. Specialized Mersenne tests and practical cryptographic key generation serve different purposes.

A record outside the Mersenne family

PrimeGrid search · 2,524,1902,097,152 + 1 · 13,426,224 digits

The result. This generalized Fermat prime is listed sixth overall in the checked PrimePages table, making it the largest listed non-Mersenne prime in that snapshot. The entire base is raised to the exponent: it is not 2,524,190 multiplied by a power of two.

The wider lesson. Large-prime research is not one single race. Different algebraic forms permit different tests, proof strategies, and hardware optimizations. Searching only the easiest special forms tells us little by itself about how arbitrary large primes are distributed.

Questions still
driving the field.

OPEN

Riemann and L-functions

Understand zero locations, zero-free regions, zero density, and related L-functions. The Riemann Hypothesis concerns all nontrivial zeros; computations checking many zeros and theorems controlling some region are important but different achievements.

Clay Mathematics Institute: problem statement.

OPEN

Twin primes and prime tuples

Bounded-gap theorems established that infinitely many primes have neighbors within a fixed finite distance. The unconditional bound 246 does not prove infinitely many gaps of size 2. More generally, Hardy–Littlewood heuristics predict the frequency of admissible prime patterns, accounting for congruence obstructions.

Polymath8b research account.

OPEN

Goldbach and additive structure

The even two-prime conjecture remains open in this guide's checked snapshot. The odd three-prime theorem is proved. Analytic tools study how primes can sum to targets; large computations test finite ranges and supply data for conjectured formulas.

Published finite verification · Try small cases.

OPEN

Primes in thin sequences

Are infinitely many numbers n² + 1 prime? We do not know. Proving infinitely many primes in a sequence can get much harder when the sequence occupies a sparse subset of the integers. Results about arithmetic progressions do not automatically apply to quadratic sequences.

Related programs study digit restrictions, polynomial values, and primes represented by more complicated forms. Precise hypotheses matter more than a suggestive picture.

PROVED LANDMARK

Patterns without periodicity

The Green–Tao theorem proves primes contain arithmetic progressions of every finite length. It does not say all terms of one infinite arithmetic progression are prime, nor that the primes eventually repeat a fixed pattern.

Green and Tao: arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.

ONGOING COMPUTATION

Searches, certificates, and reproducibility

Distributed prime searches, exact prime counting, record gaps, and independently checkable certificates all remain active. A reproducible computation should document its bounds, code, verification strategy, and the status of each endpoint or candidate.

A research literacy checklist

Before sharing
“a breakthrough.”

  1. Find the original statement. A headline may omit “almost all,” “assuming,” or “sufficiently large.”
  2. Check publication status and date. A preprint is not automatically peer reviewed; a new version may change a claim.
  3. Identify the output. Is it a proof, a conditional theorem, a finite verification, a heuristic, or an unverified claim?
  4. Compare like with like. “A prime exists” and “the correct asymptotic number of primes exists” are different targets.
  5. Ask what remains open. A zero-density improvement is not a proof of RH; a record prime is not a largest possible prime.
  6. Keep the source attached. Read the theorem, hypotheses, and limitations before repeating an AI-generated or journalistic summary.

This desk is a curated, dated set of notes. It does not claim to monitor every new paper or validate every proposed proof. Unverified sensational claims are intentionally excluded.
