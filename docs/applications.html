FROM EVERYDAY ARITHMETIC TO DIGITAL SYSTEMS

Small building blocks.
Real-world impact.

Primes organize multiplication, make finite-field division possible, and help construct useful computational problems. Their value is much broader—and more specific—than “primes keep the internet safe.”

Start with the purpose / Beginner

Three reasons primes
keep appearing.

01 / DECOMPOSITION

Explain multiplication

Every integer greater than 1 has unique prime ingredients. This turns questions about divisibility, fractions, GCDs, and repeating cycles into questions about matching factors.

In a recipe, scaling 12 portions to 18 uses a 3/2 factor. Prime factorization explains which factors cancel; you do not need a huge prime to benefit from number theory.

02 / ALGEBRA

Make division reliable

Modulo a prime p, every nonzero residue has an inverse. You get a finite field: a small arithmetic universe in which addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by nonzero values behave consistently.

That consistency is what applications often need—not the mysterious appearance of prime digits.

03 / COMPUTATION

Separate easy from hard

Multiplying two large integers is practical; recovering their factors can be much harder. Exponentiating in a suitable finite group is practical; reversing it may require solving a hard discrete-logarithm problem.

Cryptographic security depends on exact constructions, parameter sizes, implementation, and assumptions—not merely choosing a prime.

How are the two exponents connected?

For distinct primes, φ(n) = (p − 1)(q − 1). Choose e coprime to φ(n), then find d with ed ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)). This demo tries e = 17, otherwise a small valid odd exponent. The inverse exists because e and φ(n) are coprime.

Why does decryption recover the message?

For a message coprime to n, Euler's theorem gives mφ(n) ≡ 1 (mod n), so raising m to ed = 1 + kφ(n) gives m back. For messages sharing a factor with n, analyze the result separately modulo p and modulo q, then use the Chinese Remainder Theorem. RSA's round trip still works for every residue m modulo n.

Is factoring exactly the same problem as breaking RSA?

Factoring n gives p and q, which lets an attacker compute the private exponent. It is therefore sufficient to break RSA. But a general equivalence between factoring and inverting every RSA instance is not known. Real deployments can also fail through padding mistakes, side channels, reused randomness, or compromised endpoints.

Do websites encrypt entire pages directly with RSA?

No. Modern secure connections combine mechanisms. Public-key methods support authentication or key establishment; efficient symmetric ciphers protect bulk traffic. TLS 1.3 removed static RSA key exchange, though RSA signatures can still authenticate certificates or handshake messages. Elliptic-curve and post-quantum mechanisms are also important.

Protocol details: TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446) and PKCS #1 / RSA (RFC 8017).

A prime-sized field

Modulo 7, the inverse of 3 is 5 because 3 × 5 ≡ 1. Thus “divide by 3” means “multiply by 5.” Modulo 8, 2 has no inverse: 2 times any integer is even, never congruent to 1.

This is why linear equations and polynomial interpolation are particularly well-behaved over fields. Explore the multiplication table and clock.

QR codes and damaged data

Reed–Solomon codes add carefully chosen redundancy so a receiver can recover data even when some symbols are wrong or missing. QR codes use this idea: the extra symbols are structured mathematical information, not copies of the original message.

QR-code arithmetic uses a field with 256 elements, GF(2⁸). This is not the integers modulo 256. Its elements can be represented by binary polynomials, with multiplication reduced by an irreducible polynomial.

Secret sharing

In a threshold scheme, any chosen number of shares can reconstruct a secret, while fewer shares reveal nothing under the scheme's assumptions. A polynomial of degree t − 1 is determined by t suitable points over a field.

Shamir's construction hides the secret as a polynomial's constant term and chooses the other coefficients randomly. Finite-field interpolation recovers it. Secure randomness and correct implementation are essential.

Elliptic curves

Points satisfying an elliptic-curve equation over a finite field can form a group. Adding a point to itself many times is efficient; recovering the multiplier from the result is the elliptic-curve discrete-logarithm problem.

The field's size and the group's order are different quantities. A prime field does not automatically make the entire point group have prime order; secure parameters select appropriate groups or subgroups.

Deeper note: why do finite fields have prime-power sizes?

A finite field has prime characteristic p: adding 1 to itself p times gives zero, and a composite characteristic would create zero divisors. The field is a vector space of some finite dimension k over its prime subfield, so it has pk elements. Conversely, a field exists for every prime power, unique up to isomorphism. Arithmetic modulo a composite integer is usually a ring, not a field.

Beyond encryption

Primes in the
computational toolbox.

Hashing and fingerprints

A polynomial hash can treat a string as coefficients and evaluate it modulo a prime. Field structure helps analyze collisions and randomized equality tests. The prime does not make collisions impossible.

Not every hash table needs a prime capacity, and a rolling hash is not automatically a cryptographic hash. Cryptographic hashes such as SHA-256 have different designs and security requirements.

Fast polynomial multiplication

A number-theoretic transform (NTT) is a Fourier-like transform performed in modular arithmetic. Choose a modulus with suitable roots of unity—for a length L transform, often a prime p with L dividing p − 1.

This permits exact modular convolution and is useful in polynomial arithmetic, large-integer computation, and some lattice-based cryptographic implementations.

Independent clocks and reconstruction

The Chinese Remainder Theorem says that remainders modulo pairwise coprime moduli determine a unique residue modulo their product. For example, x ≡ 2 (mod 3) and x ≡ 3 (mod 5) together give x ≡ 8 (mod 15).

The moduli need not themselves be prime; coprimality is what matters. This can split a large arithmetic problem into smaller ones and recombine the answers.

Distributed search as engineering

Record-prime projects stress-test exact arithmetic, processors, GPUs, software reliability, and verification procedures. Independent confirmations help distinguish a mathematical result from a hardware error.

The largest known Mersenne prime is not a normal RSA key ingredient. Record hunting and practical cryptographic prime generation have different goals.

Changing applications / Standards update

What happens
after quantum computers?

A sufficiently capable fault-tolerant quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could factor integers and solve relevant discrete logarithms efficiently. That threatens RSA and conventional elliptic-curve systems. It does not mean primes stop existing, become predictable, or cease to be useful.

The 2024 NIST milestone

On August 13, 2024, NIST released its first three finalized post-quantum standards: ML-KEM for key encapsulation, ML-DSA for signatures, and SLH-DSA for hash-based signatures.

These do not simply “use bigger RSA primes.” They rely on different mathematical foundations. Some lattice-based constructions still use modular arithmetic and NTT-friendly primes, but their security assumptions are not integer factorization.

NIST's original announcement. This is a standards milestone, not a prediction of when cryptographically relevant quantum hardware will exist.

Beginner takeaway: what should I actually do?

Learn the concepts here; use maintained, reviewed cryptographic libraries in real applications. Follow current protocol and standards guidance rather than selecting primes or composing cryptographic primitives yourself. The educational RSA demo above is designed to reveal its secrets, not protect yours.
